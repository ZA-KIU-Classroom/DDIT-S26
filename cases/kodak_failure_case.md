# Case: Kodak and the Invention Trap

## Source
Christensen, Clayton, *The Innovator's Dilemma*
Publicly available business reporting, 1975 to 2012

## Purpose
Use this case to understand why invention without transformation produces no competitive advantage. Apply the Three Horizons framework to diagnose what Kodak should have done differently. Then apply the same logic to your client.

---

## The core argument

Kodak invented the digital camera in 1975. It filed for bankruptcy in 2012. The invention was not the problem. The problem was that Kodak had no operating model to commercialise the invention without threatening its most profitable business.

---

## The timeline

| Year | Event | Strategic implication |
|---|---|---|
| 1975 | Kodak engineer Steve Sasson invents the first digital camera | Technology is available 37 years before bankruptcy |
| 1975 to 1990 | Digital camera technology suppressed internally. Film revenue is too important. | R&D continues in secret but no commercial product is launched. The invention exists as a liability, not an asset. |
| 1991 | Kodak launches its first commercial digital camera | Canon and Sony have been in the market for years. Kodak enters as a follower in a market it invented. |
| 1994 to 2000 | Kodak attempts multiple digital strategies. None succeed at scale. | No digital distribution, no software competency, no direct consumer relationship. The capabilities required do not exist. |
| 2003 | Kodak announces it will stop selling traditional film cameras in the US and Europe | Too late. Digital competitors are entrenched. Kodak's cost base from film production and retail partnerships cannot be covered by digital revenue. |
| 2012 | Kodak files for Chapter 11 bankruptcy | 37 years after inventing the technology that killed it. |

---

## The three failure mechanisms

### 1. The invention trap
Kodak suppressed its own invention for 15 years to protect film revenue. When it finally launched a commercial product, competitors had already captured market positions.

Lesson: R&D without a commercialisation pathway is not a competitive asset. Invention that threatens the core business will be suppressed by the financial logic of that business unless it is ring-fenced from the P&L.

### 2. The capability gap
Kodak's core competency was chemistry, optics, and photo processing logistics. Its sales organisation was built around photo retailers. When digital arrived, Kodak had:
- No digital distribution capability.
- No software engineering competency.
- No direct consumer relationship (all sales went through retailers).
- No platform or ecosystem logic.

The assets that created film-era dominance were irrelevant to digital.

### 3. Failure to apply Three Horizons logic
What Kodak should have done in 1990:
- H3: create a ring-fenced digital business unit with a separate P&L, separate ROI hurdles, and separate management. Do not subject it to film-era performance expectations.
- H2: use H1 film profits to fund H2 digital infrastructure: software engineers, digital distribution channels, consumer data.
- H1: manage film as a declining cash cow. Optimise for cash generation, not growth. Use the cash to fund H3.

None of these decisions were made. Film P&L management prevented the investment logic that Three Horizons required.

## Application to your client

### Where is your client's film?

Every incumbent has an equivalent: the profitable business model element that makes transformation hardest to approve. For Kodak it was the film processing margin. For Blockbuster it was the late fee revenue.

Name the equivalent in your client's industry. Be specific. Quantify it if possible.

### What ring-fencing would your client require?

If your recommended H3 initiative were subject to the same ROI hurdles as the H1 core business, would it survive the annual budget process?

If the answer is no, your recommendation must include a ring-fenced budget structure for H2 and H3 initiatives. This is not optional. It is the governance condition that makes transformation possible.

### What capabilities are missing?

The operating model section of your final project must name the capabilities your recommended transformation requires that your client does not currently have. Then it must state how your client acquires them: build, buy, or partner.
